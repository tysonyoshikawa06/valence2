# VSEPR and Hybridization

---

## What are VSEPR and Hybridization?

**VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion)** theory predicts the **three-dimensional shape** of molecules based on one principle: electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible.

**Hybridization** explains molecular geometry at the quantum level. Atomic orbitals on the central atom **mix** to form new hybrid orbitals that point in the directions predicted by VSEPR.

Together, these two models let you predict and explain the shape, bond angles, and bonding of virtually any molecule you encounter in AP Chemistry.

---

## VSEPR Theory

### Electron Domains

An **electron domain** is any region of electron density around the central atom:

- Each **single bond** = 1 domain
- Each **double bond** = 1 domain (counts the same as a single bond for geometry)
- Each **triple bond** = 1 domain
- Each **lone pair** = 1 domain

> Only count domains on the **central atom**.

### Electron Domain Geometry vs. Molecular Geometry

- **Electron domain geometry**: arrangement of ALL electron domains (including lone pairs)
- **Molecular geometry**: arrangement of only the **bonded atoms**

Lone pairs are invisible in the molecular shape, but they still push on bonded pairs.

### Effect of Lone Pairs on Bond Angles

Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs because they are attracted to only one nucleus instead of two. This causes:

- Lone pairs compress bond angles
- More lone pairs = smaller actual bond angles than the "ideal"

**Example: Comparing H₂O, NH₃, and CH₄ (all tetrahedral electron geometry)**

| Molecule | Lone Pairs | Bond Angle |
| -------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| CH₄      | 0          | 109.5°     |
| NH₃      | 1          | 107°       |
| H₂O      | 2          | 104.5°     |

## Hybridization

### What is Hybridization?

Atomic orbitals (s, p, d) on the central atom **combine** to form new **hybrid orbitals** of equal energy. The number of hybrid orbitals formed equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed.

The type of hybridization matches the number of **electron domains**:

| Electron Domains | Hybridization | Geometry             |
| ---------------- | ------------- | -------------------- |
| 2                | sp            | Linear               |
| 3                | sp²           | Trigonal planar      |
| 4                | sp³           | Tetrahedral          |
| 5                | sp³d          | Trigonal bipyramidal |
| 6                | sp³d²         | Octahedral           |

---

### Sigma and Pi Bonds

- **Sigma (σ) bond**: formed by **head-on** overlap of orbitals — one per bond (single, double, or triple)
- **Pi (π) bond**: formed by **side-by-side** overlap of unhybridized p orbitals — additional bonds in double and triple bonds

| Bond Type | σ bonds | π bonds |
| --------- | ------- | ------- |
| Single    | 1       | 0       |
| Double    | 1       | 1       |
| Triple    | 1       | 2       |

> Every bond has one sigma bond. Only double and triple bonds have pi bonds.

## Examples

### Example 1: Determining Geometry of H₂O

Lewis structure: O with 2 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs

- Electron domains on O = 4
- Electron geometry = tetrahedral
- Molecular geometry = **bent**
- Bond angle ≈ **104.5°** (compressed by 2 lone pairs)
- Hybridization = **sp³**

---

### Example 2: BF₃

Lewis structure: B with 3 bonding pairs, 0 lone pairs

- Electron domains = 3
- Electron geometry = trigonal planar
- Molecular geometry = **trigonal planar**
- Bond angle = **120°**
- Hybridization = **sp²**

Note: B only has 6 electrons — it is an exception to the octet rule.

---

### Example 3: CO₂

Lewis structure: O=C=O, two double bonds, no lone pairs on C

- Electron domains on C = 2 (each double bond is one domain)
- Electron geometry = **linear**
- Molecular geometry = **linear**
- Bond angle = **180°**
- Hybridization = **sp**
- Bonds: 2 σ bonds (C to each O) + 2 π bonds (one per double bond)

---

### Example 4: Counting Sigma and Pi Bonds in a Molecule

For ethylene (H₂C=CH₂):

- C–H bonds: 4 sigma bonds
- C=C bond: 1 sigma + 1 pi bond
- Total: **5 σ bonds, 1 π bond**

---

## Polarity

A molecule is **polar** if:

1. It has polar bonds (electronegativity difference > 0.4), **AND**
2. The bond dipoles do **not cancel** due to asymmetric geometry

Examples:

- CO₂: polar bonds but **linear** → dipoles cancel → **nonpolar molecule**
- H₂O: polar bonds and **bent** → dipoles do not cancel → **polar molecule**

---

## Why It Matters

VSEPR and hybridization explain:

- The **3D shape** of molecules, which determines **reactivity and properties**
- Why water is bent and polar (enabling hydrogen bonding)
- Why CO₂ is linear and nonpolar (affecting its behavior as a solvent/gas)
- The relationship between **orbital mixing** and **bond geometry**
- **Molecular polarity**, which governs intermolecular forces (studied next unit)

---

## Common Mistakes

- Counting double bonds as two domains (they count as one)
- Forgetting that lone pairs affect bond angles but not molecular geometry labels
- Mixing up electron geometry and molecular geometry
- Assuming all sp³ molecules are tetrahedral (lone pairs change the molecular shape)
- Forgetting that every bond contains exactly one sigma bond

---

## AP Exam Tip

> Start every geometry problem by drawing the complete Lewis structure. Count electron domains on the central atom. Determine electron geometry first, then subtract lone pairs to get molecular geometry. Hybridization always matches the number of electron domains.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **VSEPR**: electron domains repel → minimize repulsion → determines geometry
- **Lone pairs** compress bond angles below ideal values
- **Hybridization** = sp, sp², sp³, sp³d, sp³d² based on number of electron domains
- **Sigma bonds** = all bonds (head-on overlap); **Pi bonds** = double/triple only (side-overlap)
- Molecular **polarity** depends on both bond polarity and geometric symmetry

# Structure of Metals and Alloys

---

## What is the Structure of Metals?

Pure metals are held together by **metallic bonds** — a unique bonding model in which metal atoms release their valence electrons into a shared, delocalized "sea" of electrons. The positively charged metal cations (called **kernels**) are embedded in and attracted to this electron sea.

This model explains almost every unusual property of metals: they conduct electricity and heat, they can be hammered into sheets, they can be drawn into wires, and they have a characteristic metallic luster.

---

## The Metallic Bonding Model

In a metal:
- Each atom contributes its valence electrons to the collective pool
- Electrons are **delocalized** — they are not associated with any single atom
- The electron sea holds the positive kernels together through electrostatic attraction
- Kernels are arranged in a regular crystal lattice

> Think of it as positive ions floating in a sea of electrons that act as "glue."

---

## Crystal Packing Structures

Metal atoms pack together in regular arrangements to minimize potential energy. The three most common arrangements are:

### Face-Centered Cubic (FCC)
- Atoms at each corner and the center of each face of the unit cell
- Coordination number = 12
- Very efficient packing (~74% space filled)
- Examples: Cu, Ag, Au, Al, Ni

### Body-Centered Cubic (BCC)
- Atoms at each corner and one atom in the center of the cube
- Coordination number = 8
- Less tightly packed (~68% space filled)
- Examples: Fe, Cr, W, Mo

### Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP)
- Similar packing efficiency to FCC (~74%)
- Coordination number = 12
- Examples: Mg, Zn, Ti

> FCC and HCP are the most efficiently packed structures — atoms take up the most space.

## Properties of Metals (and Why)

| Property | Explanation |
|----------|-------------|
| Electrical conductivity | Delocalized electrons move freely to carry current |
| Thermal conductivity | Electrons transfer kinetic energy rapidly |
| Malleability | Layers of ions can slide over each other; electron sea reforms |
| Ductility | Same sliding mechanism allows drawing into wires |
| Metallic luster | Free electrons absorb and re-emit light of many frequencies |
| High melting point | Strong attraction between kernels and dense electron sea |

> Metals are malleable because the electron sea allows ion layers to shift without breaking. This is fundamentally different from ionic solids, which shatter.

## Alloys

An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (metals or nonmetals) that results in **enhanced properties**.

Alloys are not compounds — their composition can vary. The added element disrupts the regular lattice, which generally makes the material **harder and stronger**.

### Substitutional Alloys

- Atoms of one metal are **replaced** by atoms of a similar-sized metal
- The foreign atoms occupy lattice sites of the host metal
- Disrupts the lattice slightly → harder than pure metal
- Example: **Sterling silver** (92.5% Ag, 7.5% Cu), **Brass** (Cu + Zn), **Bronze** (Cu + Sn)

### Interstitial Alloys

- Smaller atoms **fit into the gaps** (interstices) between the larger host metal atoms
- Dramatically increases hardness and strength by blocking dislocation movement
- Example: **Steel** (Fe + C) — carbon atoms sit in the gaps of the iron lattice

### Why Alloys Are Stronger

In a pure metal, lattice planes can slide past each other easily. When foreign atoms are present:
- They pin the lattice planes
- Dislocations cannot propagate
- The material resists deformation → harder and stronger

> Steel is far stronger than pure iron because carbon atoms lock the iron lattice in place.

## Examples

### Example 1: Explaining Conductivity

Why do metals conduct electricity but ionic solids do not (in solid form)?

- Metals: delocalized electrons move freely throughout the structure
- Ionic solids: all electrons are localized in bonds; ions cannot move in solid state
- ✔ Mobile electrons in metals carry charge; ionic solids have no mobile charge carriers in solid state.

---

### Example 2: Comparing Malleability

Why is iron malleable but NaCl is not?

- In iron, ion layers slide over each other while the electron sea maintains cohesion
- In NaCl, shifting layers brings like charges together → repulsion → fracture
- ✔ Metallic bonding allows deformation; ionic bonding does not.

---

### Example 3: Why Steel is Stronger than Iron

Pure iron has a BCC lattice that can deform relatively easily. Adding carbon (up to ~2%):
- Carbon atoms occupy interstitial sites
- This pins dislocations in the lattice
- ✔ Steel resists deformation and is much harder than pure iron.

## Why It Matters

The structure of metals explains:

- The **physical properties** that make metals useful in engineering
- Why **alloys** are used in nearly all real-world metal applications
- The connection between **atomic-scale structure** and macroscopic behavior
- Why metals behave so differently from ionic or covalent solids

---

## Common Mistakes

- Thinking metallic bonds are weak (they are quite strong — metals have high melting points)
- Confusing malleability (sheets) with ductility (wires) — both come from the same mechanism
- Forgetting that alloys are not compounds (no fixed composition)
- Assuming interstitial alloys involve substitution (they fill gaps, not replace)

---

## AP Exam Tip

> When explaining metal properties, always reference the **delocalized electron sea**. For alloy strength, specify whether it is **substitutional** (similar-sized atoms replace host atoms) or **interstitial** (small atoms fill gaps), and explain how each disrupts dislocation movement.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Metals have a **delocalized electron sea** surrounding a lattice of positive kernels
- Common packing structures: **FCC, BCC, HCP**
- Metallic properties (conductivity, malleability, luster) all trace back to free electrons
- **Alloys** improve strength by disrupting the lattice — substitutional or interstitial
- **Steel** (Fe + C interstitial) is the most important industrial alloy example

# Photoelectron Spectroscopy

---

## What is Photoelectron Spectroscopy?

**Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES)** is an experimental technique that measures how much energy is required to remove electrons from an atom. It provides direct evidence for the **shell and subshell structure** of atoms.

Think of it like this: you shine a very high-energy light on an atom and measure how fast the ejected electrons fly out. Electrons closer to the nucleus require more energy to remove, so they leave slower. The pattern of ejection energies maps directly onto electron configuration.

PES gives you a **visual fingerprint of an atom's electron arrangement** — it is experimental proof that energy levels exist.

---

## Key Concepts

### 1. How PES Works

1. A beam of high-energy photons (X-rays or UV) strikes a sample of atoms
2. Photons transfer energy to electrons
3. Electrons with enough energy are ejected
4. The kinetic energy of each ejected electron is measured
5. Binding energy is calculated from the difference

---

### 2. The Fundamental Equation

$$
BE = hf - KE
$$

Where:

- $BE$ = binding energy (energy required to remove the electron)
- $hf$ = energy of the incoming photon
- $KE$ = kinetic energy of the ejected electron

> High binding energy → electron was held tightly (close to nucleus)
> Low binding energy → electron was loosely held (outer shell)

---

### 3. Reading a PES Spectrum

A PES spectrum plots:

- **x-axis:** binding energy (increases right to left by convention)
- **y-axis:** relative number of electrons (peak height)

Each **peak** corresponds to a different subshell.

Key rules for interpreting peaks:

- **Peak position** → binding energy → identifies which subshell
- **Peak height** → relative number of electrons in that subshell
- **Peaks further right** (higher binding energy) → closer to the nucleus

---

### 4. Connecting PES to Electron Configuration

For an atom like sodium ($1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^6\ 3s^1$):

| Subshell | Electrons | Relative Peak Height | Binding Energy |
|----------|-----------|----------------------|----------------|
| 1s       | 2         | small                | highest        |
| 2s       | 2         | small                | medium-high    |
| 2p       | 6         | tallest              | medium         |
| 3s       | 1         | smallest             | lowest         |

> Peak height ratios reflect the number of electrons: 2 : 2 : 6 : 1

## Examples

### Example 1: Identifying an Element from a PES Spectrum

A spectrum shows three peaks with relative heights 2 : 2 : 3 at decreasing binding energies.

- Total electrons: 2 + 2 + 3 = 7
- Configuration: $1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^3$
- Element: **Nitrogen**

---

### Example 2: Comparing Binding Energies

Why does the 1s peak in nitrogen have a higher binding energy than the 2s peak?

- 1s electrons are closer to the nucleus
- Coulomb's Law: force increases as distance decreases
- Greater attraction → more energy needed to remove → higher binding energy

---

### Example 3: PES and Shielding

Compare the 1s binding energy of carbon vs. neon:

- Carbon: 6 protons
- Neon: 10 protons

Neon's 1s electrons experience greater nuclear attraction because there are more protons and the 1s electrons are not shielded from them.

✔ Neon has a **higher 1s binding energy**.

---

## Why It Matters

PES provides experimental evidence for:

- The **quantized energy levels** of electrons
- The **subshell structure** (s, p, d, f)
- Why electrons in the same atom have different ionization energies
- The relationship between nuclear charge and electron binding

> PES is what makes electron configuration observable, not just theoretical.

---

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing peak height with binding energy (height = number of electrons, not energy)
- Reading the x-axis in the wrong direction
- Forgetting that core electrons have higher binding energy than valence electrons
- Assuming equal-height peaks mean equal binding energies
- Confusing PES with atomic emission spectra

---

## AP Exam Tip

> On the AP exam, you may be given a PES spectrum and asked to identify the element or write its electron configuration. Count the electrons from each peak (proportional to height), then match to a known configuration.

---

## Key Takeaways

- PES measures the **binding energy** of electrons in each subshell
- Higher binding energy = electron closer to the nucleus
- Peak height = relative number of electrons in that subshell
- PES spectra are direct experimental evidence for electron shell structure
- The pattern of peaks maps directly onto the atom's electron configuration

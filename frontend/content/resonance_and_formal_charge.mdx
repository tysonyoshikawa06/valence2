# Resonance and Formal Charge

---

## What are Resonance and Formal Charge?

When drawing Lewis structures, some molecules cannot be represented accurately by a single structure. **Resonance** describes molecules where electrons are delocalized across multiple bonds, producing a **hybrid** that is an average of several valid Lewis structures.

**Formal charge** is a bookkeeping tool that assigns a hypothetical charge to each atom in a Lewis structure, assuming electrons are shared equally. It is used to determine which Lewis structure best represents reality.

---

## Lewis Structures: A Quick Review

Before resonance, recall how to draw a Lewis structure:

1. Count total valence electrons
2. Connect atoms with single bonds
3. Satisfy the octet of outer atoms with lone pairs
4. If the central atom has fewer than 8 electrons, form double or triple bonds
5. Verify that total electrons match the count from step 1

---

## Resonance

### Why Resonance Exists

Some molecules have electrons that are not confined to a single bond. Instead, they are **delocalized** — spread across several atoms. No single Lewis structure can capture this correctly, so we draw multiple **resonance structures** and understand the real molecule as a **resonance hybrid**.

### Key Rules

- Resonance structures differ only in the **position of electrons** (bonds and lone pairs)
- The **atoms never move** between resonance structures
- The actual molecule is the **weighted average** of all resonance structures
- Resonance structures are connected by a **double-headed arrow** (↔)
- Delocalized electrons lead to **greater stability** (lower energy)

### Example: Ozone (O₃)

Ozone has two equivalent resonance structures:

- Structure 1: O=O–O (double bond on left)
- Structure 2: O–O=O (double bond on right)

The real ozone molecule has **both O–O bonds equal in length** — intermediate between a single and double bond. Neither structure alone is correct; the hybrid is.

---

### Resonance Hybrid

The actual molecule is represented as the **resonance hybrid**:

- Bond lengths are **equal** between equivalent positions
- Electron density is **spread out** over the molecule
- The more resonance structures possible, the **more stable** the molecule

> Benzene (C₆H₆) is the classic example: 6 equivalent resonance structures produce a perfectly symmetric ring with equal bond lengths of ~140 pm, intermediate between C–C (154 pm) and C=C (134 pm).

## Formal Charge

### Definition

Formal charge is the charge an atom would have if **all bonding electrons were shared equally**.

$$
FC = V - N - \frac{B}{2}
$$

Where:

- $V$ = valence electrons of the neutral atom
- $N$ = nonbonding (lone pair) electrons on that atom
- $B$ = bonding electrons shared with that atom

### Interpreting Formal Charge

- $FC = 0$ → atom has "normal" electron assignment
- Positive $FC$ → atom has fewer electrons than expected
- Negative $FC$ → atom has more electrons than expected

### Rules for the Best Lewis Structure

1. **Minimize formal charges** — the best structure has formal charges closest to zero
2. **Negative FC on the more electronegative atom** — the atom that pulls electrons should be the one carrying negative charge
3. **Adjacent same-sign FC are unfavorable** — avoid having two adjacent atoms both positive or both negative

---

## Examples

### Example 1: Formal Charge on CO₂

For carbon dioxide (O=C=O):

- Carbon: $V = 4$, $N = 0$, $B = 8$ → $FC = 4 - 0 - 4 = 0$
- Oxygen: $V = 6$, $N = 4$, $B = 4$ → $FC = 6 - 4 - 2 = 0$

✔ All atoms have $FC = 0$ — this is the best structure.

---

### Example 2: Choosing Between Structures for SCN⁻

The thiocyanate ion can be drawn three ways. Calculate formal charges to find the best structure:

For [:S=C=N:]⁻:

- S: $FC = 6 - 2 - 3 = +1$ (unfavorable — positive on electronegative S)

For [:S–C≡N:]⁻:

- S: $FC = 6 - 6 - 1 = -1$, C: $FC = 4 - 0 - 4 = 0$, N: $FC = 5 - 2 - 3 = 0$

✔ The structure with the triple bond on the more electronegative nitrogen end minimizes formal charges and places negative charge appropriately.

---

### Example 3: Resonance in Carbonate (CO₃²⁻)

Carbonate has three equivalent resonance structures:

- One C=O double bond and two C–O single bonds (three orientations)

The real carbonate ion has:

- All three C–O bonds **equal in length**
- Bond length intermediate between C–O single (~143 pm) and C=O double (~122 pm)
- The negative charge **delocalized** over all three oxygens

## Why It Matters

Resonance and formal charge explain:

- Why some molecules are **more stable** than simple Lewis structures suggest
- Why **bond lengths** in delocalized molecules don't match predicted values
- How to choose the **most accurate** Lewis structure when multiple are possible
- The structure and reactivity of important molecules like **benzene, ozone, and carbonate**

---

## Common Mistakes

- Moving atoms (not just electrons) between resonance structures
- Forgetting to account for lone pairs when calculating formal charge
- Thinking resonance structures are real, distinct molecules (they are not)
- Choosing a structure based on appearance rather than formal charge calculations
- Forgetting that the formula $FC = V - N - B/2$ uses bonding electrons, not pairs

---

## AP Exam Tip

> Always calculate formal charges when the problem asks for the "best" Lewis structure. Show your work using $FC = V - N - B/2$ for each atom. Structures with all formal charges of zero are preferred; if unavoidable, negative FC should be on the more electronegative atom.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Resonance** occurs when electron delocalization means no single Lewis structure is fully correct
- The real molecule is the **resonance hybrid** — an average of all valid structures
- Delocalized electrons make molecules **more stable**
- **Formal charge** = $V - N - B/2$ for each atom
- The best Lewis structure **minimizes formal charges** and places negative FC on electronegative atoms

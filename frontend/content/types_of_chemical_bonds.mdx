# Types of Chemical Bonds

---

## What are Chemical Bonds?

A **chemical bond** is a force of attraction that holds atoms together in a compound. Bonds form because bonded atoms are at a lower potential energy than unbonded atoms — nature favors stability.

The **type of bond** that forms depends primarily on the **electronegativity difference** between the atoms involved. Electronegativity is the tendency of an atom to attract electrons toward itself.

---

## The Three Main Bond Types

### 1. Ionic Bonds

**Ionic bonds** form by the complete **transfer of electrons** from a metal to a nonmetal.

- Metal loses electrons → forms **cation** (positive ion)
- Nonmetal gains electrons → forms **anion** (negative ion)
- Opposite charges attract → ionic bond

Electronegativity difference: typically **greater than 1.7**

Properties of ionic compounds:
- High melting and boiling points
- Brittle crystalline solids
- Conduct electricity when dissolved or melted
- Do not conduct electricity as a solid

Example: $\text{Na}^+ + \text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{NaCl}$

---

### 2. Covalent Bonds

**Covalent bonds** form by the **sharing of electrons** between nonmetals.

Electronegativity difference: typically **less than 1.7**

#### Nonpolar Covalent

- Electrons are shared **equally**
- Electronegativity difference ≈ 0 (same or very similar elements)
- Examples: H₂, O₂, Cl₂, N₂

#### Polar Covalent

- Electrons are shared **unequally**
- One atom pulls electron density more strongly
- Creates a **partial negative (δ-)** and **partial positive (δ+)** end
- Electronegativity difference between 0.4 and 1.7
- Examples: H₂O, HCl, NH₃

> The greater the electronegativity difference, the more polar the bond.

---

#### Bond Order

Covalent bonds can involve sharing of one, two, or three pairs of electrons:

| Bond Type | Pairs Shared | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Single    | 1           | H–H     |
| Double    | 2           | O=O     |
| Triple    | 3           | N≡N     |

Higher bond order → **shorter bond length** and **higher bond energy**

---

### 3. Metallic Bonds

**Metallic bonds** exist in pure metals and alloys.

- Metal atoms release their valence electrons into a shared "sea"
- Positive metal cations are embedded in this electron sea
- Delocalized electrons hold the lattice together

Properties this explains:
- **Electrical conductivity** — electrons move freely
- **Thermal conductivity** — electrons transfer heat
- **Malleability and ductility** — layers can slide without breaking bonds

---

## Electronegativity and Bond Type Summary

| EN Difference | Bond Type        |
|---------------|------------------|
| 0 – 0.4       | Nonpolar covalent |
| 0.4 – 1.7     | Polar covalent    |
| > 1.7         | Ionic             |
| Metal atoms   | Metallic          |

> These cutoffs are guidelines, not hard rules. Bond character exists on a continuum.

---

## Bond Energy and Bond Length

$$
\text{Bond Energy} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Bond Length}}
$$

- Stronger bonds are **shorter** and require **more energy to break**
- Triple bonds > double bonds > single bonds in both energy and shortness

---

## Examples

### Example 1: Classifying a Bond

Is the bond in HF ionic or covalent?

- Electronegativity of H ≈ 2.1, F ≈ 4.0
- Difference = 1.9 → ionic by the strict cutoff, but HF is typically considered **polar covalent** in practice

> This illustrates that bond type is a spectrum, not a binary.

---

### Example 2: Bond Order and Strength

Rank in order of increasing bond energy: N–N, N=N, N≡N

$$
N\text{–}N < N{=}N < N{\equiv}N
$$

Triple bond has the highest bond energy and shortest bond length.

---

## Why It Matters

Understanding bond types allows you to predict:

- **Physical properties** (melting point, conductivity, hardness)
- **Reactivity** (which bonds break and form in reactions)
- **Molecular shape and polarity**
- **Intermolecular forces** (studied in later units)

---

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming all metal-nonmetal combinations are ionic (some are polar covalent)
- Confusing bond polarity with molecular polarity
- Thinking metallic bonds are weak (they are actually quite strong)
- Forgetting that bond order affects both length and energy

---

## AP Exam Tip

> When asked to predict bond type, calculate the electronegativity difference and match it to the table. When explaining properties, always connect them back to the specific bond type and the forces involved.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Ionic bonds**: electron transfer, metal to nonmetal, EN difference > 1.7
- **Polar covalent bonds**: unequal sharing, EN difference 0.4–1.7
- **Nonpolar covalent bonds**: equal sharing, EN difference < 0.4
- **Metallic bonds**: delocalized electron sea in metals
- Higher bond order = shorter length and higher bond energy

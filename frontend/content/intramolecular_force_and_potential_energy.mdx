# Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy

---

## What are Intramolecular Forces?

**Intramolecular forces** are the forces that hold atoms together **within** a molecule or compound. These are the actual chemical bonds — ionic, covalent, and metallic.

The prefix *intra* means "within." This distinguishes them from **intermolecular forces**, which act *between* separate molecules (covered in later units).

The strength of intramolecular forces determines the **stability** of a compound and its physical and chemical properties.

---

## Potential Energy and Chemical Bonds

### Why Bonds Form

Atoms bond because doing so **lowers their potential energy**. A lower energy state is more stable. When two atoms approach each other:

- At large distance: little attraction, high potential energy
- As they approach: attraction increases, potential energy decreases
- At the **bond length**: potential energy is at its minimum (most stable)
- Too close: electron-electron and nuclear-nuclear repulsion increases potential energy sharply

> Bonds form at the distance where potential energy is minimized.

---

### Potential Energy vs. Internuclear Distance

The relationship between potential energy and the distance between two bonding atoms follows a characteristic curve:

- **Far apart**: PE approaches zero (no interaction)
- **Bond length**: PE is at its lowest (most negative) point — this is the **bond dissociation energy** measured from this minimum back to zero
- **Too close**: PE rises steeply (repulsion dominates)

The depth of this energy well tells you the **bond energy**: how much energy must be supplied to break the bond.

$$
\Delta PE = \text{Bond Energy (when broken)}
$$

---

## Bond Length and Bond Energy

The two key measurables of any bond:

### Bond Length

- The **equilibrium distance** between two bonded nuclei
- Located at the minimum of the potential energy curve
- Shorter bonds are generally **stronger**

### Bond Energy (Bond Dissociation Energy)

- Energy required to **break one mole of bonds** in the gas phase
- Always **positive** (breaking bonds requires energy input)
- Higher bond energy = more stable bond

| Bond  | Bond Length (pm) | Bond Energy (kJ/mol) |
|-------|-----------------|----------------------|
| C–C   | 154             | 347                  |
| C=C   | 134             | 614                  |
| C≡C   | 120             | 839                  |
| N–N   | 145             | 163                  |
| N≡N   | 110             | 945                  |

> As bond order increases, bond length decreases and bond energy increases.

---

## Bond Order and Stability

**Bond order** is the number of bonding pairs between two atoms.

$$
\text{Bond order} = \frac{(\text{bonding electrons}) - (\text{antibonding electrons})}{2}
$$

For simple Lewis structures:
- Single bond → bond order 1
- Double bond → bond order 2
- Triple bond → bond order 3

Higher bond order → deeper potential energy well → more stable bond → more energy needed to break it.

---

## Ionic Bonds and Potential Energy

In ionic compounds, potential energy is minimized when ions are arranged in a **crystal lattice** — the geometry that maximizes attractions and minimizes repulsions.

**Lattice energy** is the energy released when gaseous ions come together to form an ionic solid. It reflects the depth of the potential energy well for ionic bonds.

$$
U \propto \frac{q_1 \cdot q_2}{r}
$$

Where:
- $q_1$, $q_2$ = charges on the ions
- $r$ = distance between ion centers (sum of ionic radii)

> Higher charge and smaller ionic radius → more negative lattice energy → more stable solid.

---

## Examples

### Example 1: Comparing Bond Stability

Which bond is more stable: C=C or C–C?

- C=C has higher bond order → lower potential energy at equilibrium → more stable
- C=C has bond energy ≈ 614 kJ/mol vs C–C ≈ 347 kJ/mol

✔ The double bond is significantly more stable and harder to break.

---

### Example 2: Interpreting a PE Curve

A potential energy curve shows a minimum at –436 kJ/mol at 74 pm.

- Bond length = **74 pm** (equilibrium distance)
- Bond energy = **436 kJ/mol** (energy to return PE to zero)
- This describes the **H–H bond**

---

### Example 3: Lattice Energy Comparison

Which has greater lattice energy: NaCl or MgO?

- NaCl: charges +1 and –1, larger ions
- MgO: charges +2 and –2, smaller ions

$$
U_{MgO} \gg U_{NaCl}
$$

MgO is far more stable due to higher charges and smaller ionic radii.

---

## Why It Matters

Intramolecular forces and potential energy explain:

- Why some compounds are more **thermally stable** than others
- How much **energy is released or absorbed** during reactions
- Why **bond length and bond strength are inversely related**
- The stability of ionic, covalent, and metallic structures
- Why reactions occur — systems move toward lower potential energy

---

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing intramolecular (within molecule) with intermolecular (between molecules)
- Thinking higher bond energy means the bond is longer
- Forgetting that bond formation releases energy (PE decreases)
- Assuming all bonds of the same type have the same energy

---

## AP Exam Tip

> When comparing stability of compounds or bonds, refer to the **depth of the potential energy well**. A deeper well = stronger bond = more energy needed to break it. For ionic compounds, use the charge-to-radius ratio from Coulomb's Law.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Intramolecular forces are bonds **within** a molecule
- Bonds form at the distance where **potential energy is minimized**
- Higher bond order → shorter bond length → higher bond energy → more stable
- Lattice energy of ionic solids increases with higher ion charge and smaller radius
- Bond energy equals the depth of the potential energy well
